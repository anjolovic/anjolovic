**Business logic, read plainly:** Bright Side is a *volume* play. Cheap-to-produce, evergreen, globally legible, kid-safe, ad-friendly, and topic-agnostic. Every structural choice below serves retention and the mid-roll ad slot, not depth.

### 1.2 The scenario families (content pillars)

Every Bright Side video falls into one of roughly seven buckets. Each has its own title grammar and its own retention mechanic.

| # | Family | Title grammar | Retention mechanic | Example shapes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **What-If hypotheticals** | "What If [impossible premise]?" | Escalation ladder: minute 1 → hour 1 → day 1 → year 1 | *What If You Never Stopped Growing?*, *What If the Moon Vanished?* |
| 2 | **Survival / "what to do if"** | "How to Survive…", "X Tips If…", "You Have 60 Seconds…" | Countdown clock + numbered tips | *13 Tips on How to Survive Wild Animal Attacks* |
| 3 | **Riddles & tests** | "Only 1% Can…", "X Riddles That Will…" | Pause-timer interaction; viewer competes | Survival riddles, "spot the difference," "who's lying?" |
| 4 | **Mysteries & history** | "The Real Reason…", "Scientists Can't Explain…" | Withheld answer; reveal at the end | Titanic, Bermuda Triangle, pyramids, lost civilizations |
| 5 | **Space & science explainers** | "Why…", "What's Inside…", "How Far Can…" | Scale comparisons (school buses, Olympic pools) | *How Far Can You Go Without Sleeping?* |
| 6 | **Body & psychology** | "What Happens to Your Body If…", "Signs That…" | Second-person: "your heart…", "your brain…" | Sleep, fasting, cold showers, screen time |
| 7 | **Life hacks / "you're doing it wrong"** | "X Things You've Been Doing Wrong" | Reversal per item: belief → correction | Kitchen, car, phone, travel |

The big hitters combine two families. This script combines **#1 (What-If)** with **#2 (Survival)**, which is the same DNA as their most-viewed video.

### 1.3 Hook anatomy (first 15 seconds)

Bright Side does not use an intro card or "hey guys." The narrator starts talking before the viewer's thumb has left the screen. Six recurring openers:

1. **Second-person cold open.** "You're standing on the top deck. The wind's picking up. And then…" Viewer is the protagonist within one sentence.
2. **The impossible number.** "Every year, about twenty people do this by accident. Only a handful come home." Number first, context second.
3. **The ticking clock.** "You've got about sixty seconds to make one decision." Sets a deadline the whole video pays off against.
4. **The everyday twist.** Ordinary object or moment, then a fact that makes it strange.
5. **The authority gap.** "Scientists still can't agree on why…" Manufactures a question only the video can close.
6. **The reversal.** "Everything you've heard about X is wrong." Only used when there is a real correction to deliver.

Each opener ends with an **explicit promise**: "…and by the end of this video you'll know exactly what to do." That line is the retention contract.

### 1.4 Body structure (the beat sheet they reuse)

```
0:00  HOOK           cold open + promise                       (12–18 s)
0:18  SETUP          normalize the premise; who this happens to (45–60 s)
1:15  ACT 1          the first 60 seconds of the scenario      (2 min)
3:15  MINI-CLIFF     "but that's not the worst part…"          (3 s)
3:20  ACT 2          the next hour; fact carousel begins       (3 min)
6:20  MINI-CLIFF     "…and this is where most people get it wrong"
6:25  ACT 3          the long game; hope enters                (2.5 min)
9:00  PAYOFF         checklist recap; the ONE thing to remember (1 min)
10:00 BONUS FACT     one surprising extra to reward completers  (30 s)
10:30 OUTRO          like/share/next-video CTA                  (15 s)
```

**Rules baked into the structure:**

- **A new fact or image every 20–40 seconds.** Never more than two sentences on one idea without a visual change.
- **Mini-cliffhangers at each act break**, timed near mid-roll ad positions (roughly every 3–4 minutes).
- **Escalation, not lists.** Even "13 tips" videos are ordered from mild to severe so the stakes climb.
- **Recap before the outro.** Bright Side re-states the 3–5 things to remember; this both serves comprehension and pads the watch-time in the final minute.
- **Bonus fact after the recap.** Rewards viewers who stay past the natural stopping point.

### 1.5 Narration voice

- **Second person, present tense.** "You," "your," "you're," relentlessly. The viewer is never watching someone else.
- **Warm, slightly goofy uncle.** Corny puns, "yikes," "yep," "buddy," "no thanks," mock-sighs. Never sarcastic at the viewer's expense.
- **Numbers translated into objects.** Meters become "about a 10-story building." Speeds become "faster than a highway car." Every abstract number gets a physical anchor.
- **Short sentences. One clause. Then another.** Reading level ~grade 6. Zero jargon without an immediate plain-English gloss.
- **Rhetorical questions as connective tissue.** "So what do you do now?" "Sounds bad, right?" These are the audio equivalent of a jump cut.
- **Kid-safe by design.** No gore, no profanity, danger is real but never graphic. This is what keeps the channel globally monetizable.

### 1.6 Visual & audio grammar

- **Footage:** stock clips (Shutterstock/Storyblocks style), cut every 3–6 seconds; 2D cartoon overlays for anything the stock can't show (a body diagram, a timeline, a comparison bar).
- **Text pops:** the key number or noun of every fact appears as bold text with a "pop" SFX. Viewers with sound off still follow.
- **Recurring graphics:** a timeline bar across the top for what-if videos; a countdown clock for survival videos; a "✓ / ✗" stamp for do/don't tips.
- **Music:** upbeat, royalty-free, mid-tempo; ducks under VO; swaps to a tense cue at each mini-cliffhanger.
- **SFX:** whoosh on scene changes, pop on text, heartbeat under danger moments, "ding" on tips.

### 1.7 Thumbnail & title packaging

- **Thumbnail:** one oversized subject, saturated colors, high contrast, 2–4 words max in a heavy sans-serif with a stroke. Frequently a red arrow or circle. Faces show exaggerated emotion (cartoon or stock).
- **Title:** 6–10 words, question or imperative, starts with the strongest word ("What If," "Why," "Never," "Scientists"). No numbers over 20 in "X things" titles (fewer items → higher completion).
- **Test cadence:** they A/B thumbnails aggressively and re-package old videos under new titles.

### 1.8 What to copy and what to skip

**Copy:** second-person cold open, promise line, escalation ladder, fact-every-30-seconds, mini-cliffhangers at ad breaks, number-to-object translation, recap + bonus fact, text pops.

**Skip:** invented statistics and "scientists say" without a source. The channel's growth predates YouTube's current scrutiny of low-quality educational content. A new channel gets punished for what Bright Side got away with in 2018. Every number in the script below has a verification item in Part 4.

## Part 3 — The Script

Format: `[TIME]` · **VO** (read as written) · *VISUAL* · `TEXT:` on-screen pop · `SFX:` cue.

### COLD OPEN — 0:00 to 0:18

**VO:** It's the last night of your cruise. You're on deck twelve, leaning on the railing, watching the ocean go by. The ship's doing about twenty knots. Then the deck rolls, your foot slips, and suddenly there's nothing under you but forty meters of air. And then water.

*VISUAL: Stock: cruise ship at night, deck railing POV. Hard cut to black on "nothing under you." Splash SFX.*

`SFX: wind → silence → heavy splash`

**VO:** By the end of this video, you'll know exactly what happens in your first sixty seconds in the water, why the ship can't just stop, and the one rule that decides whether you go home.

`TEXT: 60 SECONDS`

---

### SETUP — 0:18 to 1:15

**VO:** First, let's get one thing out of the way. This does happen. Not often, but often enough that the cruise industry tracks it. Roughly a couple dozen people go overboard from cruise ships every year, worldwide. And here's the part nobody puts in the brochure: only about one in five of them is ever pulled out alive.

*VISUAL: Cartoon ship with tiny figure icons; 5 figures, 4 fade to gray.*

`TEXT: ~1 IN 5 SURVIVE`

**VO:** Why so few? It's not sharks. It's not even drowning, at least not at first. It's math. It's temperature. And it's the fact that a cruise ship is basically a floating city that takes a very long time to turn around.

**VO:** So let's rewind. You're falling. Forty meters. That's about a thirteen-story building. Your fall takes just under three seconds, and you hit the water at close to ninety kilometers an hour. Roughly highway speed.

*VISUAL: Split-screen: building with floor count ticking up vs. figure falling. Speedometer needle sweeping to 90.*

`TEXT: 40 m ≈ 13 FLOORS` · `TEXT: ~90 km/h`

**VO:** At that speed, water doesn't feel like water. It feels like the ground. So how you land matters more than anything else you'll do today.

`SFX: mini-cliff sting`

---

### ACT 1 — THE FIRST MINUTE — 1:15 to 3:15

**VO:** Rule number one: feet first. Legs together, arms crossed over your chest, chin tucked. Think of it as making yourself into a pencil. Belly-flop from this height and, well, let's just say you'd be skipping the rest of the video.

*VISUAL: Cartoon figure snaps into "pencil" position. ✓ stamp. Then a starfish-pose figure with an ✗ stamp.*

`TEXT: FEET FIRST · ARMS CROSSED · CHIN DOWN`

**VO:** You hit. You go deep, maybe four or five meters. Your ears pop, everything's dark, and your brain is screaming one word: up.

**VO:** And now the real danger starts. Not the fall. The temperature.

**VO:** Even in warm-looking water, your body freaks out. The moment cold water hits your skin, you gasp. It's automatic. You can't stop it. Scientists call it cold shock, and if your face is underwater when that gasp happens, that's a lungful of ocean.

*VISUAL: Cartoon body diagram: skin → nerves → lungs, with a big involuntary "GASP" bubble.*

`TEXT: COLD SHOCK`

**VO:** For the next sixty seconds, your breathing goes haywire. Fast, shallow, uncontrollable. Your heart rate spikes. People panic here, and panic is what drowns them. So here's the whole job for minute one: float, and breathe.

**VO:** That's it. Don't swim. Don't chase the ship. Don't shout yet. Lean back, let your legs come up, and count your breaths until you can slow them down. Survival experts have a name for this window. They call it the "one" in the one-ten-one rule. One minute to get your breathing under control.

*VISUAL: Countdown clock 60 → 0. Figure on its back, breathing bubbles slowing.*

`TEXT: 1 MINUTE: CONTROL YOUR BREATHING`

**VO:** Meanwhile, up on the ship, something you didn't expect is happening. The ship isn't stopping. It can't. A hundred-thousand-ton vessel at twenty knots needs well over a kilometer just to come to a halt. And by the time anyone on board even notices you're gone, you're already far behind it.

*VISUAL: Top-down animation: ship moving forward, tiny dot receding. Distance counter ticks: 100 m… 300 m… 600 m…*

`TEXT: 20 KNOTS ≈ 10 METERS PER SECOND`

**VO:** Ten meters every second. So a minute of you catching your breath? The ship's gone six hundred meters. Six football fields. Sounds bad, right?

**VO:** But that's not the worst part.

`SFX: mini-cliff sting · music drops to tense cue`

---

### ACT 2 — THE FIRST HOUR — 3:20 to 6:20

**VO:** The worst part is that you might not have been seen at all.

**VO:** Most cruise ships rely on people to notice a fall. Cameras exist, but someone has to be watching the right screen. Some newer ships have sensors along the hull that trigger an alarm the moment something person-sized goes over the side, but as of the mid-2020s, that's still far from universal.

*VISUAL: Hull with a red laser grid; then a "?" over an older ship without one.*

`TEXT: OVERBOARD SENSORS: NOT ON EVERY SHIP`

**VO:** So let's play the odds. Let's say a passenger saw you fall. Here's what happens in the next ten minutes, and it's actually pretty impressive.

**VO:** Someone yells "man overboard." The bridge is called. A lifebuoy gets thrown, and here's a detail people miss: it has a light on it, and often a smoke marker. That's not for you. That's for the ship. It marks the spot where you went in, so the crew has a reference point in a very big, very blank ocean.

*VISUAL: Orange ring hitting water, strobe light, smoke plume.*

`TEXT: THE LIFEBUOY MARKS THE SPOT`

**VO:** Then the ship does something called a Williamson turn. The captain swings the wheel hard one way, then hard the other, and the ship loops back onto its own track in the opposite direction. It's like a giant U-turn that lands the ship right on the line it came from.

*VISUAL: Top-down diagram: ship path tracing the classic Williamson loop back onto its wake.*

`TEXT: WILLIAMSON TURN`

**VO:** Sounds great. The catch? For a ship this size, that maneuver takes somewhere around ten to fifteen minutes. And you, buddy, are a head the size of a coconut in water the color of a parking lot.

**VO:** Which brings us to the next ten minutes of your life. Remember one-ten-one? The "ten" is ten minutes. In cold water, that's roughly how long your arms and legs keep working properly. After that, your muscles get too cold to swim, to grab a rope, or even to hold on.

*VISUAL: Body diagram with arms and legs fading from red to blue.*

`TEXT: 10 MINUTES OF USEFUL MOVEMENT`

**VO:** So spend those ten minutes wisely. First: get anything off that's dragging you down. Shoes. A heavy jacket. Second, and this one surprises people: if you're wearing a jacket that's not heavy, keep it on. It traps a thin layer of water against your body and slows down heat loss. Third: if that lifebuoy landed anywhere near you, that's your entire world now. Get to it.

*VISUAL: Three quick tips with ✓ stamps. Shoes sinking; jacket with trapped-water layer; buoy.*

`TEXT: DITCH THE WEIGHT · KEEP THE LAYER · GET TO THE RING`

**VO:** Now for the biggest question: what's the water temperature? Because this changes everything.

**VO:** Caribbean cruise? The water's around twenty-seven degrees Celsius. Warm. You could float for the better part of a day before cold becomes your main problem. Your real enemies there are exhaustion, swallowing salt water, and sunburn.

*VISUAL: Thermometer graphic at 27°C, sunny palette.*

`TEXT: 27°C: HOURS TO A DAY+`

**VO:** Alaska cruise? North Atlantic crossing? That water might be ten degrees or colder. And now the "one" at the end of one-ten-one kicks in. One hour. That's roughly how long you have before hypothermia makes you lose consciousness. In some cases less.

*VISUAL: Thermometer plunging to 10°C, palette shifts cold blue. Clock: 60 min.*

`TEXT: 10°C: ~1 HOUR`

**VO:** Here's the thing, though. You don't actually die from cold in that hour. You pass out, and then you drown. Which is why the number one thing that saves people in cold water isn't swimming ability. It's flotation.

`SFX: mini-cliff sting`

**VO:** And this is where most people get it wrong.

---

### ACT 3 — GETTING FOUND — 6:25 to 9:00

**VO:** Instinct says: swim. Swim toward the ship. Swim toward land. Swim anywhere. Wrong.

**VO:** Swimming burns heat about thirty to forty percent faster than staying still. And unless you can see a shore you could actually reach, you're trading an hour of survival for a few hundred meters of progress. Bad deal.

*VISUAL: Two figures side by side. Swimmer's heat bar drains fast; floater's drains slow.*

`TEXT: SWIMMING = FASTER HEAT LOSS`

**VO:** Instead, do what search-and-rescue divers do. Pull your knees up toward your chest, hug them with your arms, and keep your head out of the water. It looks a bit silly. It's called the HELP position, short for Heat Escape Lessening Posture. You're protecting the places where you lose heat fastest: your armpits, your groin, and your chest.

*VISUAL: Cartoon figure curling into the HELP position, warm zones highlighted.*

`TEXT: H.E.L.P. POSITION`

**VO:** Now, how does the ship find a coconut in a parking lot?

**VO:** Once the ship's back on track, every spare crew member is on deck with binoculars. Lifeboats get lowered, and those move much faster and sit much lower than the ship, which matters, because from a lifeboat you can actually see a head in the swell. Coast guards get called. Nearby ships get called. And they all search a pattern, in expanding boxes, starting from that lifebuoy.

*VISUAL: Top-down animation: expanding search pattern squares centered on the buoy's strobe.*

`TEXT: EXPANDING SQUARE SEARCH`

**VO:** Your job is to be visible. Waves hide you. So when you see a boat or hear an engine, raise one arm high and wave it. Just one. Wave both, and you push yourself under. If you've got anything bright, a hat, a shirt, hold it up. And if you can whistle, whistle. Sound carries over water better than your voice does.

*VISUAL: Figure waving one arm ✓, both arms ✗ (dunks). Whistle icon with sound waves.*

`TEXT: ONE ARM · SOMETHING BRIGHT · MAKE NOISE`

**VO:** What about at night? This is the nightmare version. Most falls happen at night, and at night a searchlight is the only thing that finds you. So get back to that strobe. If you can reach the lifebuoy, you're not just floating. You're a blinking light in a black ocean. That's the difference.

*VISUAL: Night scene, tiny strobe blinking in a huge dark frame. Searchlight beam sweeping in.*

**VO:** Oh, and the sharks? Statistically, not your problem. Sharks aren't cruising open ocean waiting for cruise passengers. Cold, exhaustion, and not being seen are what actually get people. Sorry, movies.

*VISUAL: Cartoon shark shrugging.*

`TEXT: SHARKS: NOT THE PROBLEM`

**VO:** And what if the ship never saw you? Then it's about the next ship, or the coast guard, or daylight. People have been found alive after ten, fifteen, even twenty hours in warm water. In cold water, the window is far shorter. Which is why everything, everything, comes back to the same three words.

---

### PAYOFF — RECAP — 9:00 to 10:00

**VO:** Float. Don't swim.

*VISUAL: Big bold title card.*

`TEXT: FLOAT. DON'T SWIM.`

**VO:** Let's run it back. If you ever find yourself going over the side of a very large boat:

**VO:** One. Feet first, arms crossed, chin tucked. Survive the landing.

`TEXT: 1. FEET FIRST`

**VO:** Two. Your first minute is for breathing, not swimming. Cold shock passes. Panic doesn't have to.

`TEXT: 2. BREATHE FOR 60 SECONDS`

**VO:** Three. Use your ten good minutes to ditch heavy stuff and reach the lifebuoy.

`TEXT: 3. GET TO THE RING`

**VO:** Four. Pull into the HELP position and stop burning heat.

`TEXT: 4. H.E.L.P. POSITION`

**VO:** Five. Be seen. One arm, something bright, make noise, stay near the light.

`TEXT: 5. BE SEEN`

**VO:** And the honest truth? The best move is the boring one. Stay behind the railing. Don't climb it for a photo. Don't lean over it after a couple of drinks. Nearly every one of these cases starts on the wrong side of a railing that was doing its job just fine.

*VISUAL: Railing with a ✓ stamp. Warm, calm music returns.*

## Part 5 — Retention Notes (why each beat exists)

- **0:00 no intro card.** Bright Side loses no seconds to branding. The channel name is the watermark.
- **0:12 promise line.** Names three specific payoffs. Viewers who want any one of them stay.
- **0:30 the "1 in 5" stat.** Establishes stakes before the viewer has decided whether this is a joke video.
- **1:15 first act break** lands the first tip ("feet first") in under 90 seconds so the viewer feels a return on time immediately.
- **3:15 / 6:20 mini-cliffs** sit within a few seconds of where mid-rolls will be placed. The cliff carries the viewer over the ad.
- **Temperature fork (5:20)** is the "personalization" beat: the viewer maps the scenario to *their* cruise, which raises comment volume ("I'm going to Alaska in June, thanks a lot").
- **Shark beat (8:10)** is placed late on purpose. It's the question everyone has; answering it late keeps them.
- **Recap (9:00)** converts a 10-minute video into something shareable. "Send this to someone booking a cruise" is the share hook.
- **Bonus fact (10:00)** rewards completion and lifts average view duration past the natural drop-off at the recap.
- **End screen CTA** names a specific next video (pilot passes out) rather than "check out my channel."
