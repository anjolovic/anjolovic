### COLD OPEN — 0:00 to 0:18

**VO:** You're in fourteen C. Window seat, seven hours into a nine-hour flight, somewhere over the Atlantic. The cabin's dark. The seatbelt sign chimes on, which is normal. Then a flight attendant walks past you toward the front, fast. Not coffee fast. Something's-wrong fast. And up front, behind a locked door, the captain has just slumped forward onto the controls.

*VISUAL: Night cabin, POV from 14C. The purser passes at a near-jog. Hard cut to the cockpit: the captain's head dropping, the first officer's hand snapping to the yoke.*

`SFX: seatbelt chime → cabin hum → a single hard cut to silence`

**VO:** By the end of this video, you'll know exactly what happens, and exactly what to do.

`TEXT: 35,000 FEET`

### SETUP — 0:18 to 1:15

**VO:** First, the honest version. This happens more often than you'd guess, and it matters less than you'd fear. In one American study covering six years of airline flying, pilots became seriously unwell at the controls a handful of times a year. Stomach bugs, fainting, heart trouble. And in a survey of thousands of pilots, roughly a quarter admitted they'd been at least briefly out of action in flight at some point in their careers. Mostly, again, the stomach.

*VISUAL: Diagram stage. A row of tiny aircraft icons, one lights up red per year. A pie chart, mostly "stomach."*

`TEXT: A HANDFUL PER YEAR`

**VO:** So why haven't you heard about it? Because almost none of them make the news. And the reason is the whole point of this video. There are two of them up there.

*VISUAL: Cockpit, wide. Two seats, two people, two sets of controls. The door behind them, closed, the lock light on.*

`TEXT: TWO PILOTS. ALWAYS.`

**VO:** Thirty-five thousand feet is about ten and a half kilometres up. The air outside is around minus fifty, and you're doing close to nine hundred kilometres an hour. And the plane is being flown, right now, by nobody. It's on autopilot. Which turns out to be very good news.

`TEXT: -50°C · ~900 KM/H · AUTOPILOT ON`

`SFX: mini-cliff sting`

### ACT 1 — THE FIRST SIXTY SECONDS — 1:15 to 3:15

**VO:** Back to the cockpit. The captain's down. The first officer, the other pilot, says two words out loud: "I have control." It sounds theatrical. It's a trained reflex, and it settles the only question that matters in that second: who is flying the plane.

*VISUAL: First officer, close. Lips move, eyes on the instruments. Right hand rests on the thrust levers.*

`TEXT: "I HAVE CONTROL"`

**VO:** Then, in order. Check the autopilot's engaged. It is. Push the captain's seat back on its rails so a slumped body can't shove the controls. Pull the shoulder harness tight around them. And if there's any chance the problem is the air, get the oxygen mask on. Total time, about the length of this sentence.

*VISUAL: Quick cuts, hands only: seat rails, harness buckle, the mask coming down from the overhead panel.*

`TEXT: SEAT BACK · HARNESS · MASK`

**VO:** Then a call to the cabin. Not "help." Something calmer, a code phrase, or just "purser to the flight deck." That's the walk you saw from fourteen C.

**VO:** Now here's the bit that isn't on the safety card. The cockpit door has been locked since the wheels came up, and it stays locked. So the crew use the two-person rule. One flight attendant guards the galley, one goes in. Nobody else gets near that door.

*VISUAL: The galley curtain. Purser at the door keypad, a second crew member blocking the aisle with a trolley. The lock light goes green.*

`TEXT: TWO-PERSON RULE`

**VO:** Inside, the flight attendant helps drag the captain out of the seat. Harder than it sounds in a room that small. Then first aid. Most airlines carry a medical kit and a defibrillator. And then the announcement you've probably heard once or twice: "If there's a doctor on board." There usually is.

*VISUAL: Cabin, a passenger three rows back unbuckles and stands.*

`TEXT: "IS THERE A DOCTOR ON BOARD?"`

**VO:** Meanwhile the first officer's talking to the ground. Medical emergency declared. Nearest airport with a long runway and a hospital picked. Plane turned. You'll feel a gentle bank and a change in engine note. That's all. Landing a big jet alone is something every airline pilot trains for in the simulator, over and over, precisely because of days like this.

*VISUAL: Top-down map. The route bends toward a coastline. A runway icon lights up.*

`TEXT: ONE PILOT CAN LAND IT`

**VO:** So from fourteen C, the whole thing looks like this. A chime. A fast walk. A turn. An early landing at an airport you didn't book, and a lot of ambulances on the tarmac. Sounds fine, right?

**VO:** But that's not the worst part.

`SFX: mini-cliff sting · music drops to tense cue`

### ACT 2 — THE VERSION PILOTS ACTUALLY FEAR — 3:20 to 6:20

**VO:** The worst part is the version where it isn't a heart attack. Where it isn't one pilot. Where it's both.

**VO:** Only one thing takes out two healthy people in the same room at once, and it's the air. At cruise, the cabin is pumped up to feel like a mountain town, about two thousand metres. If that pressure fails, you're breathing the real outside air. And at thirty-five thousand feet, the useful time you have before your brain stops making good decisions is somewhere between fifteen and thirty seconds.

*VISUAL: Diagram stage. Altitude column on the left. A clock beside 35,000 ft reads 0:15 to 0:30. Beside 25,000 ft it reads 3 to 5 minutes.*

`TEXT: 35,000 FT: 15–30 SECONDS`

**VO:** Fifteen seconds. That's why your oxygen mask drops by itself. It's not waiting for you to work it out.

**VO:** This isn't hypothetical. In August two thousand and five, a Boeing seven thirty-seven took off from Cyprus with a hundred and twenty-one people on board. A cabin-pressure switch had been left in the wrong position after maintenance. As the plane climbed, the cabin quietly stopped holding its air. A warning horn sounded, and the crew mistook it for a different one. About thirteen minutes after take-off, both pilots were unconscious.

*VISUAL: Daylight, a 737 climbing. Cockpit: a warning horn, a hand reaching for the wrong panel. The two pilots' heads lower, slowly.*

`TEXT: HELIOS 522`

**VO:** And here's the part that stays with people. The plane didn't crash. Not then. The autopilot flew it exactly where it had been told, all the way to Athens, then round and round in a holding pattern for hours. Fighter jets came up alongside and saw the cockpit empty. It only came down when the fuel ran out.

*VISUAL: Two fighter jets in formation beside a silent airliner. Through the cockpit window, an empty seat.*

`TEXT: THE PLANE FLEW ITSELF FOR HOURS`

**VO:** So what actually protects you from that? Three things, and you already know the first one.

**VO:** One. The masks. When they drop, you have those fifteen to thirty seconds, so yours goes on before you help anyone else. You're no use to a child if you've passed out. The mask only makes oxygen for about a quarter of an hour, which sounds short until you hear the next bit.

*VISUAL: Masks dropping. A parent pulling their own on first, then a child's.*

`TEXT: YOUR MASK FIRST`

**VO:** Two. The dive. The moment the pressure goes, the pilots get the plane down to ten thousand feet, where you can breathe, as fast as it safely can. Steep, loud, deliberate. It feels like the end of the world. It's the opposite. And it takes a few minutes, which is why your mask gives you a quarter of an hour.

*VISUAL: Cabin tilted, masks swinging, the passenger in 14C gripping the armrest. Altitude readout unwinding fast: 35,000… 25,000… 10,000.*

`TEXT: EMERGENCY DESCENT TO 10,000 FT`

**VO:** Three. Redundancy, everywhere. Two pilots. Many airlines serve them different meals so one bad prawn can't get both. And after a tragedy in twenty fifteen, where a pilot locked his colleague out, a lot of airlines brought in a rule that there must always be two people on the flight deck. If one goes down, someone's there to open the door.

*VISUAL: Two meal trays, different food. The purser stepping onto the flight deck as the captain steps off to the toilet.*

`TEXT: TWO IN THE COCKPIT`

**VO:** Which brings us to the question everyone actually wants answered. Both pilots out. Crew's in. Autopilot's on. Could you, fourteen C, walk up there and land it?

`SFX: mini-cliff sting`

**VO:** And this is where most people get it wrong.

### ACT 3 — COULD YOU LAND IT? — 6:25 to 9:00

**VO:** Most people think yes. They've seen the film. They've played the game. Someone will talk them down, and there'll be applause.

**VO:** On an airliner, the honest answer is almost certainly not. Not because the plane can't land itself. Big jets can. It's called autoland. The catch is that it has to be set up by someone who knows which of several hundred buttons to press, in what order, and it needs a runway with special equipment on the ground. Get one step wrong and you've got a very expensive glider.

*VISUAL: Cockpit overhead panel, POV of someone who's never seen it. Hundreds of switches. Slow push in.*

`TEXT: AUTOLAND: NEEDS A PILOT TO SET IT UP`

**VO:** There is a plane that lands itself at the push of one button. It's real. If the pilot goes quiet, it can work out on its own that something's wrong, pick an airport, talk to air traffic control in a synthetic voice, fly there, and land. But it lives in small planes. Six-seat turboprops. Little business jets. Not the seven thirty-seven. Not yet.

*VISUAL: A small jet's panel. A single red guarded button labelled EMERGENCY AUTOLAND. A finger lifts the guard.*

`TEXT: ONE BUTTON. SMALL PLANES ONLY.`

**VO:** So what about the small plane? What if you're not on an airliner at all? Because that's where the story everyone's thinking of actually happened.

**VO:** May twenty twenty-two. A single-engine Cessna off the coast of Florida, coming home from a fishing trip. Three aboard. The pilot says he doesn't feel well. Then he's gone, slumped, hands off the controls. A tear in the main artery out of his heart, the kind of thing that kills you on an operating table, let alone in the air.

*VISUAL: Bright daylight, a small white turboprop over blue water. Inside: the pilot's head drops. The plane's nose follows.*

`TEXT: MAY 2022`

**VO:** A passenger who'd never flown a plane in his life climbs over three rows of seats into the cockpit. The plane is in a steep dive toward the ocean. He hauls the pilot aside, gets his hands on the controls, pulls back, and levels it out. Then he finds the headset, and the button that lets him talk.

*VISUAL: The passenger scrambling forward, then a close up: a hand finding the push-to-talk switch on the yoke.*

`TEXT: PUSH TO TALK`

**VO:** On the ground, a controller who also happened to be a flight instructor got called off his break. He'd never flown that plane. So he printed out a picture of its cockpit and, over the radio, flew it through a man who didn't know what any of the buttons did. Turn here. Push that. Slower. They landed at Palm Beach. The controller called him the best student he'd ever had.

*VISUAL: A tower, a printout of an instrument panel on the desk, a hand tracing it. Cut to the Cessna touching down, wobbling, holding.*

`TEXT: LANDED. EVERYONE WALKED AWAY.`

**VO:** And the pilot? Nine hours of surgery. He lived. Because a passenger kept the plane in the air long enough for someone to get to him.

**VO:** So here's what that story teaches. Not that you can land a plane. That you can talk to someone who can. Every plane has a radio. Every headset has a button. And the plane, left alone, mostly wants to fly straight and level. Your whole job is to stop making it worse, and get a voice in your ear.

`TEXT: DON'T FLY IT. TALK.`

## Part 4 — Retention Notes (why each beat exists)

- **0:00 no intro card.** The purser's fast walk is the hook; the viewer already knows that walk.
- **0:12 promise line.** Fixed brand line, names two payoffs.
- **0:30 "a handful a year."** Establishes that the premise is real, then immediately defuses it with "two of them up there." The tension isn't whether you die; it's what the version you should fear looks like.
- **1:15 first act break** lands "I have control" inside 90 seconds so the viewer feels immediate return on time.
- **3:15 / 6:20 mini-cliffs** sit at mid-roll positions. The first flips the premise from "one pilot" to "both." The second flips it from "could the plane land itself" to "could you."
- **Helios (4:00)** is the single named accident. It's placed early in Act 2 so the reassurance beats (mask, dive, redundancy) come after the fear, not before.
- **Mask-first beat (5:00)** is the personalisation moment. Every viewer has ignored that briefing.
- **"Could you land it?" (6:25)** is the question the title promised. Answering the airliner version honestly (no) before the small-plane version (yes, with help) makes the true story land harder.
- **Push-to-talk (7:50)** is the one physical instruction in the episode. It's the equivalent of "float, don't swim."
- **Recap (9:00)** converts to shareable: "send this to the nervous flyer in your life."
- **Bonus (10:00)** rewards completion with the twist that inverts the whole premise: the plane already knows.
- **End screen** points at the freezer episode, which already exists and shares the Survival playlist.
